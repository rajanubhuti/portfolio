OVERVIEW

Redesigning DPDS TMS

Tata Motors' Digital Product Development System team runs its internal ticketing on a tool that hasn't kept pace with how the department has grown. My brief: redesign it, and add an AI layer that helps people find answers before they need to file anything at all.

WHAT I INHERITED

The form wasn't broken. It just couldn't tell people apart.

*Current Ticketing Management System Homepage

I filed test tickets myself, then sat with staff while they filed real ones. The test tickets showed the obvious friction. The real sessions showed the actual problem: a hardware fault and a forgotten password were going through the same four field form.

FOUND

Navigation as dumping ground

Too much crammed into the navbar, no hierarchy.

FOUND

One form fits none

A password reset takes the same fields as a hardware failure.

FOUND

No visual hierarchy

Blue boxed nav with no distinction between primary and secondary actions.

FOUND

Zero self service

No knowledge base check before a ticket gets filed, for anything.

WHAT I DID ABOUT IT

Beyond the redesign itself, I ran a full navigation and information architecture audit: what's grouped where, what sits at the top level, and what's buried. The actual structure is confidential under Tata Motors' NDA.

KEY INSIGHT

Enterprise leaders intercept issues with self service before a ticket ever gets filed. TATA TMS had no "before," only a form.

TWO PEOPLE, SAME TICKET

The form doesn't know who's talking

Same submit button, two unrelated problems: one person is reporting a fault that could stall a line, the next just wants their badge reactivated. The form asks both of them for a severity level.

TECHNICAL STAFF

Filing a real fault and wants precision: severity, location, timestamp, and a way to track it closely.

“VPN connection dropping every 30 minutes on home network.”

EVERYONE ELSE

Wants one thing fixed, like a password or a badge, and has no patience for an engineer's form.

“Can't log in, think my password expired.”

INSIGHT → DECISION

One form can't serve both without failing one of them. That ruled out a traditional ticket form. Instead, the interaction adapts to what the person says, rather than asking them to interpret a field first.

THE FIRST ATTEMPT

Built for the person who already knew the system

A queue, status counts, filters and a table: the version I'd have wanted as a power user. Then I walked it past the same non technical staff from my earlier sessions. They came in wanting to say what was wrong, not scan a queue for it. It was the wrong front door rather than the wrong feature. The queue still mattered for tracking and for technical staff. It just couldn't be the first thing everyone saw.

THE PIVOT

Ask what's wrong, instead of teaching the system first

WHY A CONVERSATION, NOT A FORM

People already describe problems in plain language to a colleague or helpdesk, not in categories and dropdowns. So the interaction was redesigned to match that.

FIRST LOAD

No form and no category picker, just a prompt and a few likely starting points. The first thing anyone sees is a question instead of a queue.

DESCRIBING THE ISSUE

The user types their VPN problem in their own words. The assistant offers a knowledge base guide first, and only surfaces a ticket once that hasn't worked, pre filled with a category and description pulled from the conversation.

WHERE MY SCOPE ENDS

Matching the query against the knowledge base and deciding when it's a match is engineering's logic. I designed what happens on either side of that decision: a resolved answer in chat if there's a match, an escalation with a pre filled ticket if there isn't.

Technical staff still get full precision when they need it. Everyone else gets one question instead of a form.

WHERE IT LANDS

Once raised, the ticket lands in the same queue from iteration 1. It now shows TKT-4821, the VPN issue Priya just described, with its real category and urgency instead of a blank submission.

FAILURE STATE, NOT JUST THE HAPPY PATH

Every input has a defined error state, not only a default and focus state. When the assistant can't help, it offers a second knowledge base search or a direct route to a person instead of a dead end.

THE SYSTEM UNDERNEATH

Built once, reused everywhere DPDS ships

Tata Motors' brand team gave me the brand guidelines and colours. I built the token system and the components, and audited every interactive state against WCAG AA.

WHAT I DIDN'T SOLVE

Not everything here is settled

JUDGMENT CALL

A segmentation I read, not measured

Technical staff versus everyone else is a pattern I observed. It held up under stakeholder review, but it isn't based on a real sample.

UNRESOLVED

Less human review, by design

Pre filling a ticket from chat speeds things up, but it skips the moment where a person thinks the request through.

WHERE IT STANDS

In review, ahead of the next build pass

Month 3 of 4. I presented it at the mid internship review, where the GM's feedback centered on clarity, and two or three colleagues responded well to how easy it was to use. That's early signal, not a structured usability study.

✓ VALIDATED

  • Audit, competitive analysis, two iterations
  • Pivot decision, grounded in the audit
  • Informal stakeholder review

○ NOT YET

  • Assistant flow: no structured usability sessions yet
  • Ticket deflection impact: a hypothesis, not yet measured

WHAT I'D TRACK

Hypotheses, not results. Nothing below has real numbers yet

01

Ticket deflection rate

Issues resolved by the assistant before becoming a ticket.

02

Self service resolution rate

Of attempted resolutions, how many actually stick.

03

Time to resolution

Whether pre filled ticket context speeds up handling.

A system built to cut down tickets should be judged by whether it actually does. Measuring that is the next step.
